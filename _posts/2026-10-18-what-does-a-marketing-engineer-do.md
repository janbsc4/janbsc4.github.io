Have you ever watched a marketing team on a Monday morning? Someone exports numbers from one dashboard and pastes them into a slide. Someone else checks whether last week’s leads actually reached sales. A third person resizes the same banner for the fifth channel. All of it matters. Almost none of it needs to be done by hand.

Finance figured this out long ago and built itself analysts who live in models and data pipelines. Product has always had engineers sitting next to it. Sales got RevOps and, more recently, the GTM engineer, whose job is to build the machine that finds and contacts prospects. Marketing, as [Nick Lafferty points out](https://nicklafferty.com/blog/marketing-engineer/), was the odd one out.[^1]

The marketing engineer is marketing’s answer. My goal here is to make the case that the role is best understood not by the tools it builds, but by what it does to results per employee.

## More output per person

A marketing engineer sits inside the marketing team and turns repetitive work into systems. Some of those systems give people hours back. Others let the team do things that used to be out of reach for its size, like maintaining localized content for a market that once needed its own local team. Either way, each person on the team gets more done.

At [Service Club](https://serviceclub.com), where I work, we launch several markets at once. We can only do that because we rely heavily on systems built by marketing engineers.

## Judgment is the scarce part

Leverage cuts both ways. An automation that sends the wrong message to the wrong people doesn’t fix the mistake. It repeats it, at scale. [Purple Horizons](https://purplehorizons.io/blog/what-is-a-marketing-engineer) describes the role as a builder half and a marketer half, and the marketer half matters more: it decides what is worth building, what is good enough to ship, and where a human has to sign off before anything reaches a customer.

Much of that judgment comes from proximity. A marketing engineer needs to understand in detail how the marketers on their team actually work: which steps they repeat, where they lose time, which workarounds they have quietly invented. Only then does what they build fit the team’s real needs instead of an outsider’s idea of them. Someone outside the marketing team, however skilled, would struggle to get that right. Sitting inside marketing is what makes the role special, and what makes it necessary.

When I [directed Haoly]({{ '/i-directed-app' | relative_url }}), my Chinese flashcard app, I noticed that once execution gets cheap, judgment becomes the scarce resource. When anyone can generate a hundred ad variants in a minute, knowing which three are worth running is the job. You no longer need to be a software engineer to build working tools. You do need to know what you are asking for, and to keep owning the tool after it ships, because a renamed field in the CRM can quietly break three automations.

## Results per employee

How do you know if it is working? Not by counting the tools you have piloted, but by results per employee. If nobody would notice when you switched a system off, it probably wasn’t worth building. The clearest test is a comparison: a small team with a marketing engineer should get more done per person than a bigger team without one, and ideally more in total.

That matters beyond the systems themselves, because headcount has costs that never show up in a budget. In *The Mythical Man-Month*, Fred Brooks [observed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooks%27s_law) that every new person adds communication channels: a team of *n* people has *n(n−1)/2* of them. Five people have ten. Fifteen have 105. More channels mean more meetings, more handoffs, and more things lost between them. Effort suffers too. Over a century ago, Maximilien Ringelmann found that people pulling a rope together each pulled less hard than they did alone, a pattern now known as the [Ringelmann effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringelmann_effect).

Small teams avoid much of this. They coordinate more easily, everyone can see how their work moves the result, and motivation tends to follow. So the benefit of a marketing engineer goes beyond what they build. They let a small team carry the workload of a big one without the baggage that usually comes with it.

## A new name for old work?

Partly, maybe. Plenty of SEO and marketing ops people have done this kind of work for years without the title. What has changed is how far one person can now take it.

I have argued that a marketing engineer is best measured by results per employee. Their systems give each person on the team more reach, their judgment makes sure that reach points somewhere useful, and together that lets a small team do the work of a big one, without becoming one.
